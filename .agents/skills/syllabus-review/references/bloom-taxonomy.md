# Cognitive levels, and what they cost to assess

Reference material. Nothing here is policy — your university's regulations win
every time, and where they disagree with this file, say so rather than quietly
applying this.

The model stores a cognitive level on every learning outcome
(`LearningOutcome.cognitive_level`, one or more of the six below). This file is
what a skill should reason from when it has to judge whether a stated level and
the assessment that measures it agree.

## The six levels

| Level | The student can | Verbs that usually mean it | What actually evidences it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **remember** | retrieve a fact | define, list, name, recall | a closed question with one right answer |
| **understand** | restate an idea in their own terms | explain, summarise, classify, compare | an explanation in prose, or a correct classification of an unseen case |
| **apply** | use a procedure in a new situation | apply, compute, solve, demonstrate | a worked problem the student has not seen before |
| **analyze** | break something into parts and see how they relate | analyse, differentiate, attribute, diagnose | a diagnosis of a case, with the reasoning shown |
| **evaluate** | judge against criteria and defend the judgement | critique, justify, defend, select | a defended choice between real alternatives |
| **create** | assemble something new that works | design, construct, plan, produce | an artefact, plus an account of the decisions in it |

## The three traps

**A verb is not a level.** "Analyse the output of the model" can be a
remember-level task if the analysis was demonstrated in class and the student
reproduces it. What sets the level is what the student has to *do that is new*,
not the word in the outcome statement.

**Multiple choice can reach `apply`, and rarely further.** A well-built item
with an unseen case and distractors that each encode a specific misconception
genuinely tests application and can diagnose failure. It cannot evidence
`create`, and its claim on `evaluate` is weak because the alternatives were
chosen by you, not by the student. If a course states `create` outcomes and
assesses them entirely by quiz, that is a real alignment failure and worth
reporting.

**Higher is not better.** A first-year course whose outcomes are all `evaluate`
and `create` is usually a course whose outcomes were written aspirationally.
Levels should rise across a programme, not within every course.

## Distribution, as a sanity check not a rule

For a final-year undergraduate course, a plausible shape is roughly a third at
`apply`, a third at `analyze`, and the rest spread over `evaluate` and
`create`, with `remember` and `understand` present but not carrying assessment
weight of their own.

Treat a distribution far from that as a **question**, not a finding. A lab
course legitimately concentrates on `apply`. A capstone legitimately sits
almost entirely in `create`. What is worth flagging is the mismatch pattern:
outcomes declared high and assessments that only ever reach `remember`.

## What to do with a mismatch

Report both sides and let the professor choose which one is wrong. Either the
outcome overstates what the course asks — in which case the outcome should come
down — or the assessment underserves it, in which case the instrument needs
work. Both are common; only the professor knows which.

Never resolve this by rewriting the outcome. Outcomes are claims.
